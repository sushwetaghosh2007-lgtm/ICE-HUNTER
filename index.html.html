Watch the sky change.

Pick any patch of sky and watch SPHEREx’s infrared images of it play like a short film. Movers slide across the field, variable stars flicker, and every pixel hides a spectrum.

The idea

Three scrolls from raw sky to ice

01

The same patch, many dates

SPHEREx scans the whole sky and comes back to each patch about every six months. Line the visits up on one grid and a pile of snapshots becomes a film.

02

Subtract, and something lights up

Take away everything that stayed put. What is left shows up orange where an object is now and blue where it was. That pair is a mover.

03

Every pixel is a spectrum

102 colours from 0.75 to 5 µm. Dips near 3.05, 4.27 and 4.67 µm are fingerprints of water, CO₂ and CO ice, and the Ice Lab measures them.

Checked flags are masked out before stacking. The percentage is how many pixels in the current epoch carry that flag. SOURCE marks stars, so leave it unchecked.

How it works. The tool asks the IRSA archive which SPHEREx exposures cover your position, then requests small cutouts around it. Each cutout is resampled onto one north-up grid, so images taken with the telescope rotated 180° line up. The sky background, which changes with the season, is subtracted, and images of the same epoch are combined. Epochs are separated by gaps of more than 20 days.

Data: SPHEREx Quick Release spectral images via NASA/IPAC IRSA. Overlays: Gaia DR3 and AllWISE through VizieR. Pixel flag meanings follow the SPHEREx Explanatory Supplement. Aperture fluxes are not aperture-corrected and positions use a plain tangent-plane solution without distortion terms.

Analyze your own spectrum

Load a spectrum table from the SPHEREx archive tools at IRSA (its spectrophotometry tool makes a spectrum at a position you choose), or paste columns. Column names like wavelength, flux and flux error are detected, and wavelength in µm, nm or Å is recognized. Flux is rescaled to its peak. The same continuum fit and column density math runs on it, so a real sightline through a dark cloud should show the dips at 3.05, 4.27 and 4.67 µm.

How the numbers work. A straight line fitted to the flux on either side of a dip gives the no-ice continuum. Optical depth is τ = ln(continuum / flux). Summing τ over wavenumber across the dip and dividing by the band strength A gives the column: N = ∫τ dν / A, with A = 2.0×10−¹⁶ (H₂O 3.05 µm), 7.6×10−¹⁷ (CO₂ 4.27 µm) and 1.1×10−¹⁷ (CO 4.67 µm) cm per molecule.

Channels follow SPHEREx's design: six detectors with 17 linear-variable-filter channels each, resolving power 41, 35, 110 and 130 across the range. Dust reddening, stellar temperature and noise are simulated. Reported errors are statistical only, and because the window clips the band wings, water columns come out roughly 10–20% low. The simulated cloud is patterned on Taurus-like clouds, where water ice appears near Av 3.2 mag (Whittet et al. 2001). Real spectra: SPHEREx data tools (IRSA).
